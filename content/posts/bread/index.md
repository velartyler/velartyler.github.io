I make a lot of bread.
Sourdough, usually, but I'm not weird about it.

Here's a bunch of bread I've made:

{{< gallery >}}

I put the gallery there because I'm proud of it, but also so that your first impression of me as someone who makes bread isn't based on what I'm about to share in the rest of this post.  

Bread is pretty simple. It's usually 3-4 ingredients: water, flour, salt, and in many breads a leavening agent (a yeast or a fermentation starter or baking soda or whatever). You combine those in some ratio and in some particular way and heat it up.

It's hard to make _bad_ bread. You could mess up or ignore over half of a recipe and still get something edible, and likely something worth eating. It's one of the oldest prepared foods for a reason.

But to be able to reliably produce (and re-produce) an excellent loaf or a loaf with specific properties requires some measure of mastery and knowledge and a good deal of experimentation.

There's plenty to be said about testing different methods and practices at every step of the process, but I've been more interested recently in doing something like stress tests on the ingredients I use.

Here's a write up of one of the more noteworthy of these experiments:

### 1
{{< figure src="starter.jpg" width="400" >}}

In the picture above, the thing on the left is a jar of my sourdough starter. It's a goo made of flour and water and whatever wild yeast and bacteria have settled into a symbiotic culture.

The thing on the right is a can of White Monster.

I really like White Monster, I probably drink 4-5 a week on average. I don't know why I like it. I'm not even sure what flavor it's supposed to be.  If that's a character flaw it's one I'm at peace with.

I know a lot less about White Monster than about the fermentation starter. It's got caffeine in it, being an energy drink, and probably stuff like vitamins. The can reports things like: "Taurine" and "Guarana" and "L-Carnitine." Artificial sweetener of some kind. I only have a few vague ideas about these substances and any functions and effects they ostensibly have, for better or for worse.

The day I took the photo above was the most thought I've ever given the ingredients of White Monster, and in that case I was really considering whether or not they would inhibit yeast or bacteria growth.   

Because as you may have guessed by now, I baked a loaf of bread but replaced the water with White Monster.

{{< figure src="bubble.png" width="400" >}}

### 2

I didn't do this in the heat of the moment. It was premeditated. I thought about it at length, and about what adjustments would need to be made. I really just wanted to know if it would work and, if so, how it would taste.

{{< figure src="scale.jpg" width="400" >}}

The recipe was fairly standard for a sourdough hearth bread. I expected to need lower the salt content, but White Monster only has 380mg of sodium. If my math was right, that would equate to roughly 1 gram of salt (assuming ~40% sodium by mass of salt).

The only necessary change was to pour the Monster out and whisk it to help it lose some of the carbonation. One can gave me 470g of liquid, so I just drank the extra 120g to get me to 350.

I added around a teaspoon full of honey to give the yeast a fighting chance, but otherwise the recipe looked something like this:

  - 500g flour (100%)
  - 350g White Monster (70%)
  - 100g starter (20%)
  - 12g salt (2.4%)

I milled some Yecora Rojo (hard red spring wheat) for the wheat. I figured the protein content would help with gluten development in case anything about the liquid discouraged it, and figured the flavor profile aligned as well as a grain could with White Monster. I sifted the bran out and re-milled it to get a fairly high extraction flour.

### 3
I admit I was concerned after mixing.

I couldn't really take things much further than a shaggy dough, and it seemed pretty thirsty still.
{{< figure src="dough.png" width="400" >}}

Gluten ended up developing nicely after some kneading and stretching. I was surprised by how quickly it was bulk fermenting also.
{{< figure src="stretch.jpg" width="400" >}}

I shaped a loaf and scored it the only way that made sense to me. I don't think I had a lame at the time. In hindsight, holding a razor blade between my fingers feels more aligned with the ethos of White Monster anyway.
{{< figure src="score.jpg" width="400" >}}

### 4
It could've proofed longer, to be honest. I wanted to know so badly how it would turn out that I didn't care about that when I put it in the oven.

{{< figure src="bread.jpg" width="400" >}}

It baked well. I mean, it worked. That's hardly an answer to if it was worth doing. You might've been imagining how it would taste, though, while reading this.  

I would guess that, if you've had an idea about the flavor, then it's likely incorrect -- I was very much incorrect in all my speculations in the moment.

I cut it while it was still a little warm. The crumb was soft, somewhat open, and suspiciously well hydrated. Succulent, even. Somehow especially chewy despite all of this.

The flavor, though, was something I've never tasted in any baked good, much less bread.

Surprisingly salty and surprisingly sweet, in the way salted fruit is. Like a slice of watermelon with salt. Juicy, but still obviously bread.I liked it a lot. I think we finished the whole loaf before it started getting stale.

I haven't found a good way to describe the flavor that doesn't make it sound awful. The White Monster flavor took on a quality in the loaf like if gummy worms, or maybe peach rings, were caramelized and lightly salted.

After seeing this loaf work, I've made loaves by swapping the water with several more obviously palatable liquids. Pasta sauce, gravy, cereal milk, pickle juice. I have a list of things to try.

The White Monster loaf stands out still as both the biggest risk and the most rewarding. I love White Monster for what it is. I love it as an energy drink from a gas station and as an index of a variety of identities (working class, white trash, goth and emo, early 2000s Tony Hawk adjacent masculinity, chronically online transfemmes, gymbro culture), all of which are in conflict with the stereotypes about artisan bread making. I love that I baked with it. I loved how the loaf turned out.

I'll probably make it again.
